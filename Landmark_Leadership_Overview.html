01The Problem, in Plain English

Our knowledge is everywhere — and nowhere useful.

Across every part of Landmark — not just one team or one system — the same thing keeps happening: valuable knowledge gets created (a design doc, a meeting, a wiki page, a decision in a ticket) and then it basically disappears. It's technically "saved" somewhere, but nobody can easily find it, trust it, or connect it to everything else that's related. Employees waste hours re-discovering things that were already figured out. Leaders can't get a fast, honest answer to "what has this team actually been working on?" And every time we try to bring AI into the picture, we hit the same wall: an AI is only as good as the knowledge we can safely hand it.

This isn't a problem for one function to solve on its own — it shows up the same way in R&D, Ops, e-commerce, supply chain, and everywhere else. So we're deliberately keeping the problem broad, even though we'll prove it out narrow.

We broke it down into three problems, in order of what needs to be solved first:

V0
How do we collect everything we know and turn it into connected knowledge?
V1
How do we get the right knowledge to the right person, at the right level of detail?
V2
How do we let AI agents use that knowledge to actually take action, not just answer questions?

These are three stages of maturity, not three separate projects — each one builds on the last. We're starting with V0 first.

02What We're Building

Think of it as a shared "second brain" for the company — a context layer that both people and AI agents can draw on.

Instead of everyone individually searching SharePoint, Teams, and DevOps and hoping they find the right thing, we're building one place that automatically pulls that knowledge together, keeps it organized and current, and lets anyone — a person or an AI agent — ask a question and get a grounded, trustworthy answer with sources.

It's not a chatbot bolted onto a search bar, and it's not a place where people have to manually upload and maintain documents. The whole point is that knowledge flows in automatically, and gets easier to find and trust over time — not that we ask employees to do more data entry.

03What This Looks Like in Practice

A few concrete examples make this real. Here's what someone could ask once the R&D domain is loaded in.

R&D team member
"What has the R&D team been working on over the last few months?"
Answer pulled from design docs, wiki pages, and meeting notes across the team — with sources cited, not a guess.
R&D team member
"Give me more detail on the enterprise knowledge work — what's been decided so far?"
A deeper, topic-specific answer drawn only from the relevant documents and discussions, not the whole team's history.
R&D team member
"Has anyone looked at optimizing token cost?"
Surfaces any prior discussion or decisions on that exact topic, even if it's buried in a meeting transcript or a wiki page nobody remembers the name of.
Leadership (cross-function)
"What are the top topics teams are working on across the business right now?"
A leader gets a fast, aggregated view of where attention is going across functions — without chasing down individual team leads for status updates.
Ops (future domain)
"What's the standard resolution for this type of incident, and has it happened before?"
SOPs, past incidents, and tickets are knowledge-heavy and highly repetitive — making Ops one of the easiest domains to demonstrate clear, immediate value.
🔒
Setting expectations honestly: the system answers from what it has actually ingested and is grounded in cited sources — it won't invent an answer it can't support, and it won't yet know about content we haven't brought in (see Scope, below). Being clear about what it can't do yet is as important as showcasing what it can.

04Our Approach: V0 → V1 → V2

We start with capture — deliberately "dumb" at first — and layer in intelligence and governance as we go.

V0 · NOW
Capture & connect
Pull knowledge automatically from core sources into one connected store
→
V1 · NEXT
Surface to the right people
Search, rank, and filter so the right person sees the right answer
→
V2 · LATER
Agents take action
Agents use the knowledge layer to actually do things, not just answer

V0 Capture first — get knowledge flowing automatically

The single most important near-term decision: focus first on capturing knowledge automatically and at scale, rather than starting with access control or perfect filtering. The goal is to avoid a world where people have to manually upload and maintain documents one at a time — we want data flowing in on its own, from day one.

V0 is intentionally "dumb" in one specific way: it ingests broadly first. Filters, approvals, and stricter guardrails get layered on in later phases, once we can see what's actually flowing through the system and what needs tightening.

V1 Surface the right knowledge to the right people

Once content is flowing in, V1 is about making sure people (and eventually agents) get the right answer, not just an answer — combining keyword and meaning-based search, ranking by how authoritative and fresh a source is, and filtering by what the requester is allowed to see.

V2 Agents take action

Once V1 is proven, the same knowledge layer becomes the foundation for AI agents that don't just answer questions but take governed actions on Landmark's behalf (e.g. creating a ticket, updating a record) — always least-privilege, and with human approval where it matters.

⚠
A deliberate sequencing choice: identity and authorization are important and will absolutely be built — but they are not the starting layer. Making access control the first slide or the first implementation dependency risks stalling progress before anything useful exists. We build capture first, prove the value, and mature governance in step with it — not as a blocking prerequisite.

06Build vs. Open Source vs. Buy

We're evaluating three paths in parallel rather than committing to one before we've learned anything from a real POC.

🛠 Build in-house

Cost: engineering time, ongoing ownership
  • Full control over data handling, security posture, and roadmap
  • Tailored exactly to Landmark's sources and domains
  • Slowest to a working v1; we own every bug
  • Requires sustained engineering investment to maintain

🔨 Use open source

Indicative cost: $0 self-hosted · ~$16–20/seat/mo cloud · custom for enterprise tier
  • Faster to a working prototype — connectors and retrieval patterns already exist
  • No licensing cost to self-host; data stays in our environment
  • May need real customization for Landmark-specific sources/domains
  • Support depends on community/project health, not a vendor SLA

💳 Buy commercial

Cost: recurring license / seat fees
  • Fastest time-to-value; vendor handles maintenance and support
  • Enterprise-grade support and SLAs out of the box
  • Ongoing license cost that scales with usage/seats
  • Less control over data handling, roadmap, and customization

08Open Questions & What We're Still Learning

  • Relevance at ingestion. If a SharePoint folder has a mix of relevant and irrelevant documents, how does the system know what matters? Our current direction: ingest broadly first, then filter and refine — rather than trying to perfectly determine relevance upfront.
  • Some retrieval/answer behaviors are unproven until we build the POC. We won't know how well ranking, freshness-weighting, and grounding actually perform in practice until we test it on real Landmark content.
  • Expectation-setting. The deck (and any demo) needs to be explicit about what the system can answer today and what it can't — so nobody walks away thinking it already covers sources or domains we haven't built yet.

V0 — Capture, in detail

Connectors pull content from source systems on a schedule. Raw content lands first in storage; only new or changed content moves forward for processing (chunk, tag, embed, store). This 2-phase model keeps ingestion resilient — a stalled or slow processing step never causes us to lose or block on a source pull.

Recommended chunking strategy

Semantic chunking — splitting documents at natural topic/meaning boundaries rather than a fixed character/token count — is the recommended default for our mixed, long-form enterprise content (design docs, wikis, meeting transcripts):

  • Fixed-size chunking is simplest but breaks sentences and topics mid-thought, which measurably hurts retrieval accuracy on long, multi-topic documents like ours (published benchmarks show a large accuracy gap — e.g. one 2025 clinical-domain study found ~87% vs. ~13% accuracy for topic-aligned vs. fixed-size chunking on multi-topic source material).
  • Semantic chunking groups sentences/passages by meaning, preserving context and cutting far fewer ideas in half — the better default whenever documents are long, mixed-topic, or narrative-style (design docs, wikis, meeting transcripts all qualify).
  • Meeting transcripts specifically should chunk by topic/decision boundary (not fixed time windows), so a single chunk maps to one coherent discussion point rather than an arbitrary 5-minute slice.
  • Fixed-size/recursive chunking remains acceptable for short, already-focused documents (e.g. a single-topic SOP) where chunking adds little value — not every source needs the heavier semantic approach.
  • Recommendation: start with semantic chunking via LangChain's semantic chunker for SharePoint/DevOps documents and Teams transcripts, and treat this as the default unless a specific source type proves fixed-size is just as effective at lower cost during the POC.

V1 — Retrieval, in detail

Retrieval is a multi-step pipeline, not a single search-and-answer call: understand the query, run semantic and keyword search in parallel, combine the results, filter by authorization, re-rank favoring fresher and more canonical sources, and only then hand the authorized context to the LLM to generate a grounded, cited answer.
